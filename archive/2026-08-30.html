The Machine Eats the Archive

Today's issue circles the question of what gets kept and who is allowed to decide — books guillotined and scanned into training data, graves bulldozed before the killing starts, a national building code rewritten to admit knowledge it spent two centuries ignoring. The counter-argument runs through the criticism too: an organist asking for two and a half hours of your attention, two singers rebuilding a lost record from memory, a painter finally letting the body hold together.

Issue No. 3 — what survives the scan
01
Art

Fine Art

Georg Baselitz "Back Again" White Cube / Bermondsey by Sofia Hallström — Flash Art International

Hallström reads Baselitz's final show as genuinely unresolved: the gold grounds are a real departure for a painter who spent sixty years in blacks, greys and muted earth, but Baselitz himself admits the only marks that convince him now are de Kooning's gestures, which she takes as dependence rather than late invention. She notes that after decades of fragmenting the body these paintings finally let it hold together, and refuses to say whether that is a breakthrough or exhaustion dressed up as painterly strategy.

A review that declines the obituary-in-advance mode and leaves the verdict genuinely open.

Deceitful Narratives: Ai Weiwei's "Button Up!" at Factory International by Chloe Chu — ArtAsiaPacific

Chu argues that Ai denounces propaganda while running on its machinery — emotional appeal, monumental scale, deliberate oversimplification — so that works like the 25-metre History of Bombs present themselves as technical fact while advancing a moral case. She is specific about where the history bends, from the Eight-Nation Alliance flags to the Wang Family Ancestral Hall's contrived provenance, and still concludes that the ambition to test the limits of the status quo is worth something, because the struggle is all we have.

It holds a canonised dissident to the standard of accuracy he demands of states.

02
Film

Film Criticism

The Dead Screen as a Mirror: Metacinema and the Trans Gaze by Edie Barnabas — Little White Lies

Barnabas argues that the metacinema of the Trans New Wave is not a stylistic borrowing from the arthouse canon but a formal consequence of living under scrutiny: visibility paired with hostility forces a habit of watching yourself from outside, and that habit gets built into the films. Reading Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma, T Blockers and The People's Joker, she shows the recursion working as interruption — making the machinery of representation visible is how these films refuse to sit inside a history of trans depiction written by other people.

It explains a formal tic as a condition of production rather than a genre convention.

Concrete Utopia (2023): Housing, Class, and the Ethics of Exclusion by Matthew Jones — Philosophy in Film

Jones argues that the earthquake in Um Tae-hwa's film invents no hierarchy — it magnifies one, turning a deed of ownership into a passport that decides who is fed — and that the residents' vote to expel outsiders is the mechanism that matters, because procedure spreads the moral weight thin enough for ordinary people to carry it. He tracks the escalation through language, the word 'cockroaches' doing the work of removing people from the category where compassion applies, and reads the building's eventual collapse as the direct arithmetic of manufacturing enemies instead of allies.

A clean account of how decent people arrive at atrocity by following a fair-seeming process.

03
Tech & AI

Tech & AI

Inside the Warehouse Where Amazon Scans and Destroys Books for AI Training by Emanuel Maiberg — 404 Media

Maiberg tracked a shipment of rare books to Amazon's VGT3 facility in Las Vegas and got an employee inside to describe the process: books arrive new, used and from libraries, workers cut the spines off, and the loose pages go through one of roughly twenty-five machines that flip them like a cash counter before the paper is dumped in shuttles. The worker's own account is the story's centre — an operation that changed procedure daily, seemed barely managed, and was steadily reducing the number of surviving copies of things nobody had catalogued first.

The physical mechanics of turning the printed record into training data, reported from inside the room.

The Nature Of Free Will In The Age Of AI by Albert Yuan — Noema

Yuan separates two questions that determinists routinely collapse — whether causation governs matter, and whether we are answerable for what we choose — and defends the compatibilist position that the second survives the first. He does the empirical work rather than waving at it, citing Aaron Schurger's finding that Libet's famous readiness potential is neural noise and vanishes altogether once the choice actually matters, then builds freedom out of Frankfurt's hierarchy of desires: agency is your first-order wants lining up with what you reflectively endorse.

It rescues a philosophical argument that machine-decision talk has been quietly borrowing without examining.

04
Sci-Fi / Fantasy

Literary Reviews

Of the Emperor's Kindness by Chaz Brenchley by Kate Macdonald — Strange Horizons

Macdonald places Brenchley's novel in the cosy fantasy political thriller line running through Leckie's Imperial Radch, and locates its pleasure in administrative texture — correct attire, gate protocol, the timing of an audience — which makes court power concrete rather than abstract. Her sharpest point concerns the servant pages, acquired young and trained identically whatever their origin, bound rather than employed, which introduces a moral complication the comparable books do not carry, and she warns that the surface warmth conceals an utterly ruthless emperor and pages with deadly skill.

A review that identifies exactly where a comfortable book stops being comfortable.

Holding Quicksilver by Simon Blackburn — Dublin Review of Books

Blackburn takes a new collection on Bernard Williams as the occasion to argue that Williams disproved the standing charge against analytic philosophy by treating profound human questions with full rigour while remaining a genuine pleasure to read. He does not let the subject off, relaying Terence Irwin's case that Homer's Greeks plainly did possess the concept of rational self-control Williams said they lacked, and Sophie Grace Chappell's argument that Agamemnon's sacrifice is not the classical dilemma Williams made of it.

Blackburn is honest enough to publish the objections that dent his own affection for the man.

05
Music

Music Criticism

Inside "The Will of Tongues," Sarah Davachi's Organ Music Masterpiece by Jon Buckland — Bandcamp Daily

Buckland's piece turns on Davachi's refusal of the word ambient: a two-and-a-half-hour organ work scored with strings, flute ensemble, brass and Chamber Choir Ireland is not built to sit behind your day, and she frames sustained listening as an ethical responsibility rather than a demand on the audience's patience. The unexpected reference point she offers is the Butthole Surfers, admired for restraint — knowing what to take out and when to add — which she treats as the same discipline that runs through Radigue and La Monte Young.

An artist making the case for duration in a format that penalises it, in her own terms.

Zulu Queens' Isithembu finds strength in heritage, sisterhood and reinvention by Khotso Masilela — Music In Africa

Masilela reads the fourteen-track collaboration between MaWhoo and Lwah Ndlunkulu as an argument that maskandi, isicathamiya and mbaqanga are living forms rather than preserved ones, with the two singers entering a male-dominated genre deliberately and reshaping it on their own terms. He hears their difference as the record's engine rather than a flaw — Ndlunkulu rustic and earthy against MaWhoo's smoother contemporary delivery — and notes that the original sessions were lost by accident and rebuilt in the studio, which gives an album about inheritance an unplanned second layer.

Traditional-form criticism that treats reinterpretation as fidelity rather than dilution.

06
Design

Design

Sketches of Utopia From Hell by Darran Anderson — Disegno

Anderson uses Erich Mendelsohn's surviving Jewish cemetery and ritual bath in Olsztyn to argue that graves are infrastructure for collective memory, and that their destruction — necroviolence — is not an aftershock of atrocity but a rehearsal for it. He runs the pattern from pharaonic defacement and damnatio memoriae through Hardy's salvaging of the St Pancras stones as decorative spolia to razed Uighur graves, bulldozed Rohingya mass graves and desecrated Palestinian burial grounds, on the blunt principle that those who unearth and defile the dead will do it to the living.

It reads a building type nobody thinks of as design as the most consequential kind there is.

Indigenising the built environment: Where to next? by Michael Mossman — ArchitectureAU

Mossman assesses what has actually happened since First Nations performance criteria were written into Australia's national competency standards in 2021, and finds real appetite — high engagement, a lot of self-directed learning — sitting alongside survey evidence of continuing discrimination, consultation fatigue and culturally unsafe workplaces. His argument is that regulation cannot carry cultural change alone: it needs experiential learning in schools, targeted professional development, and public-infrastructure case studies, with non-Indigenous practitioners treating the work as active participation rather than support.

A rare progress report that measures a professional standard against what it has changed in practice.

07
Men's Fashion

Men's Fashion

With UNIQLO U, Lemaire Taught Fast Fashion to Grow Up by Chris Erik Thomas — Highsnobiety

Thomas argues that the decade Christophe Lemaire and Sarah-Linh Tran spent on UNIQLO U mattered less as a collection than as a proof of concept — that a designer and a mass-market retailer could sustain a long relationship rather than a one-season stunt — and that Zara, COS and Massimo Dutti chasing the same clean lines and seasonless shapes is the evidence. He reads the wave that followed, Zac Posen at GAP, Clare Waight Keller as UNIQLO's global creative director, Francesco Risso at GU, as the model's success, which makes Lemaire's exit a conclusion rather than a loss.

It treats a ten-year retail arrangement as the design decision it actually was.

Noah's Notes: Black T-Shirts Are for Weirdos by Noah Johnson — Highsnobiety

Johnson's column sets consistency against storytelling, praising a brand precisely because it is fully committed to a vision that is not at all obviously commercially viable, and reducing the whole enterprise to an instruction — just make good clothes, make them meticulously, and treat them with dignity. His most useful case is Supreme, which he argues became culturally load-bearing through the predictability of its drop ritual rather than through novelty, the opposite of what the industry tells itself about hype.

Short, opinionated menswear writing that argues from product rather than from narrative.
